What Is a Percentage?

A percentage is a way of expressing part of a whole using 100 as the base. The word itself comes from the Latin "per centum," meaning "per hundred." So whenever you see a percentage, it's telling you how many parts out of every 100 you're dealing with.

For example:

  • 10% means 10 out of every 100.
  • 25% means 25 out of every 100.
  • 100% represents the whole amount.

Percentages are useful precisely because they put different quantities on the same scale. A raise of $3,000 sounds large until you know whether your salary is $30,000 or $300,000 — the percentage tells you the real size of the change regardless of the underlying numbers. That's why percentages show up constantly in shopping discounts, interest rates, budgeting, business reporting, statistics, grading, and everyday comparisons like tips or splitting a bill.

How to Calculate a Percentage

The basic percentage formula is:

Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100

This answers the question "what percent is this part of the whole?" You divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100 to convert the resulting decimal into a percentage.

Example: If you scored 45 points out of 60 on a test:
(45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75%

Here, 45 is the part, 60 is the whole, and 75% is the result. The same formula applies whether you're comparing test scores, survey responses, budget line items, or any other part-to-whole relationship. It helps to picture the whole as "100%" no matter its actual size — a whole of 60 points and a whole of $6,000 are both 100% of themselves. The formula simply rescales that relationship onto the familiar 0-to-100 range, which is why percentages make it so easy to compare things that would otherwise be hard to line up side by side.

How to Find a Percentage of a Number

Sometimes the question runs in the opposite direction: you already know the percentage and need to find the actual amount it represents.

Result = (Percentage ÷ 100) × Number

For example, to find 20% of 150:

(20 ÷ 100) × 150 = 30

Step by step, divide 20 by 100 to get 0.2, then multiply 0.2 by 150 to get 30. So 20% of 150 is 30. The calculator above does this instantly, but it's a useful shortcut to keep in your head for quick estimates, like figuring out a discount while you're standing in a shop.

How to Calculate Percentage Increase

Percentage increase measures how much a value has grown, expressed as a share of the original value.

Percentage Increase = ((New Value − Original Value) ÷ Original Value) × 100

For example, imagine a product's price rises from $80 to $100:

((100 − 80) ÷ 80) × 100 = 25% increase

The price rose by $20, and that $20 works out to 25% of the original $80 — not of the new $100. Mixing up which number is the "original" is one of the most common mistakes people make with this formula. Percentage increase is especially useful for tracking price changes, sales growth, revenue trends, investment returns, and other business or personal-finance metrics over time.

How to Calculate Percentage Decrease

Percentage decrease works the same way, but measures how far a value has fallen.

Percentage Decrease = ((Original Value − New Value) ÷ Original Value) × 100

For example, if a price drops from $200 to $150:

((200 − 150) ÷ 200) × 100 = 25% decrease

The price fell by $50, which is 25% of the original $200. This calculation comes up constantly with discounts, sales, price reductions, and shrinking budgets or expenses. Whether an answer counts as an increase or a decrease depends entirely on which direction the value moved, so it's worth double-checking your original and new values before trusting the result.

Percentage vs Percentage Points

It's easy to confuse "percentage" and "percentage points," but the two mean different things, and mixing them up can lead to real misunderstandings.

A percentage point is simply the plain, arithmetic difference between two percentages. A percentage change measures that same gap relative to the starting number.

Example: A rate changes from 5% to 7%.
The change is 2 percentage points (7 − 5).
But the relative increase is 40%, because 2 is 40% of the original 5.

This distinction matters most in finance, economics, and news reporting, where a small change in percentage points can represent a much larger relative change — or vice versa. A headline announcing that "unemployment rose 2%" usually means 2 percentage points, which could actually be a much bigger relative jump. Always pause to check which one is being described before drawing conclusions or making comparisons.

Common Percentage Examples

Percentages appear in situations most people run into every week. A few examples make the formulas above easier to picture.

Shopping

A $60 jacket is on sale for 30% off. The discount is (30 ÷ 100) × 60 = $18, so the new price is $42.

Finance

A savings account offers a 3% annual rate. On a $1,000 balance, that's (3 ÷ 100) × 1,000 = $30 in interest over a year, before compounding is taken into account.

Business

A company's revenue grows from $50,000 to $56,000. The growth rate is ((56,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000) × 100 = 12%.

School

A student answers 38 out of 40 questions correctly. Their score is (38 ÷ 40) × 100 = 95%.

Everyday Life

Leaving an 18% tip on a $45 restaurant bill comes to (18 ÷ 100) × 45 = $8.10.

Why Percentage Calculations Matter in Finance

Percentages sit at the center of nearly every financial decision. Interest rates, savings growth, and investment returns are all expressed as percentages, which is what makes it possible to compare very different amounts of money on the same scale.

When you're reviewing a loan offer, comparing savings account rates, or checking how inflation affects your budget, a percentage calculation shows you the real impact rather than just the raw numbers. The same logic applies to business growth, fees, and discounts. Depending on what exactly you're working out, different financial situations call for tools built around the specific numbers involved.

For example, if you're weighing a fixed-term savings option, a dedicated CD Calculator can help you work out the maturity value and interest earned on a certificate of deposit, since those calculations involve a fixed term and compounding that a general percentage calculator isn't designed to handle.

A small difference in a percentage rate can add up to a large difference in actual money once it's applied to a big enough amount or stretched over a long enough time. That's why it's worth running the numbers rather than relying on a rough sense of "high" or "low," especially for decisions involving borrowing, saving, or investing over several years.

Tips for Getting Percentage Calculations Right

  • Double-check which number is the "original" before calculating a change.
  • Don't confuse a percentage change with percentage points.
  • Convert percentages into decimals carefully — divide by 100 — when calculating by hand.
  • Keep units consistent, especially when comparing money, time, or quantities.
  • Confirm whether you actually need an increase or a decrease before reading the sign of the result.
  • Round only at the final step, not in the middle of a calculation.
  • Sanity-check your result: if a "10% increase" appears to double the number, something is wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the easiest way to calculate a percentage?

Divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100. Or use the calculator on this page to skip the manual math entirely.

How do I calculate 10% of a number?

Divide the number by 10, or multiply it by 0.1. For example, 10% of 250 is 25.

How do I calculate percentage increase?

Subtract the original value from the new value, divide the result by the original value, then multiply by 100. This gives you the percentage increase.

How do I calculate percentage decrease?

Subtract the new value from the original value, divide the result by the original value, then multiply by 100. This gives you the percentage decrease.

What is the difference between percentage and percentage points?

A percentage point is a plain difference between two percentages, like going from 5% to 7% being 2 percentage points. A percentage change measures that same difference relative to the starting value, which is 40% in that example.

Can a percentage calculator calculate discounts?

Yes. A discount is just a percentage decrease. Enter the original price and the percentage off to find the amount saved and the final price.

Can percentage calculations be used for financial planning?

Yes. Interest rates, savings growth, investment returns, and budget changes are all expressed as percentages, so understanding these calculations is a genuinely useful part of everyday financial planning.
